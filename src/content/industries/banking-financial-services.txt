## The Financial Sector in Syria
~ Regulators and Market Structure.
Syria’s banking sector is regulated and supervised by the Central Bank of Syria and the Council of Money and Credit, under the applicable banking and monetary legislation. The competent authorities establish licensing, capital, prudential and governance requirements and regulate foreign exchange, transfers and payment systems within their respective mandates. Institutions licensed by the Central Bank include state-owned banks, private conventional and Islamic banks, exchange and money transfer companies, microfinance institutions and, under the electronic payment framework adopted in 2026, payment and electronic money service providers.
Insurance companies are supervised by the Syrian Insurance Supervisory Commission, while the Syrian Commission on Financial Markets and Securities regulates securities offerings, listed companies and the Damascus Securities Exchange. Anti-money laundering and counter-terrorist financing obligations apply across the sector under the supervision of the Commission for Combating Money Laundering and Terrorism Financing.
The sector is in a period of transition. The lifting of international economic sanctions on Syria has created new opportunities for restoring correspondent banking relationships and cross-border financial channels, while regulatory reform, the modernisation of banking operations and the digitalisation of payments are changing how financial services are delivered in Syria.

## Key Legal Issues for Financial Institutions
~ Where Regulation Meets Transactions.
**Licensing, ownership and governance.** Establishing a bank or financial institution, or acquiring an interest in one, requires regulatory approvals, compliance with minimum capital requirements and any applicable restrictions or conditions on foreign shareholding, and adherence to governance rules on boards, committees, internal control and related-party dealings.

**Compliance and supervision.** Financial institutions are subject to continuing obligations concerning customer due diligence, suspicious transaction reporting, banking secrecy, consumer protection and regulatory reporting, and to supervisory inspection and administrative sanctions.
**Financing, security and recovery.** Lending depends on the enforceability of security, including mortgages over real estate, pledges over movable assets and shares, and personal and corporate guarantees, and on the procedures for enforcement before the courts and execution departments. Loan portfolios affected by the years of conflict raise questions of restructuring, settlement and recovery.
**Cross-border banking.** Foreign exchange regulations, transfer approvals, correspondent banking arrangements and the due diligence and compliance expectations of foreign counterparties shape international payments, trade finance and the movement of funds into and out of Syria.
**Digital finance.** The electronic payment framework opens the sector to licensed payment service providers and electronic money institutions, and requires banks to structure their partnerships, outsourcing and customer arrangements accordingly.

## International Financial Institutions and Syria
~ Reconciling Two Regulatory Frameworks.
Foreign banks, financial groups and development finance institutions engaging with Syria must reconcile Syrian regulatory requirements with their own regulatory and compliance frameworks. We assist them in assessing the Syrian legal framework, structuring their engagement, whether through correspondent relationships, participations in Syrian banks or the financing of Syrian projects, and coordinating with their legal and compliance advisers abroad.
